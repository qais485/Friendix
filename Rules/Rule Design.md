UX Psychology Rulebook for AI Systems

This document serves as a comprehensive design manual based on the psychological principles that drive user engagement, retention, and conversion.

Section 1: Interaction \& Form Design

Rule 1: Limit Choice to Combat Decision Fatigue

Rule Name: Choice Minimalization

.

Explanation: Reducing the number of options presented to a user simultaneously to prevent cognitive overload

.

Why it matters psychologically: High-choice environments lead to "Decision Fatigue," where users become overwhelmed and choose to take no action at all rather than making a difficult choice

.

Practical implementation guidance: Audit all forms and screens to remove unnecessary fields or secondary options

. Focus the user on a single primary path

.

Good example: A booking screen showing only the most essential fields or a grocery store displaying only 6 jam flavors, which led to a 30% purchase rate

.

Bad example: A booking screen with five empty fields requiring five separate decisions before progress can be made

.

Common mistakes: Assuming more choice equals a better user experience

.

Exceptions: Professional tools where power users require multiple simultaneous controls.

Rule 2: Implement Smart Defaults

Rule Name: Default Bias Utilization

.

Explanation: Pre-filling form fields and selections with the most likely or common choices

.

Why it matters psychologically: Between 70% to 90% of users never change default settings; they interpret defaults as a "recommendation" or the "correct" choice, reducing the mental load from "filling out" to "scanning and adjusting"

.

Practical implementation guidance: Use historical data or common user behavior to pre-select fields

. Change CTA text to be informative (e.g., "12 results waiting") rather than generic (e.g., "Search")

.

Good example: An airline app pre-selecting the most common departure city or current date

.

Bad example: Empty form fields that force the user to provide every piece of data from scratch

.

Common mistakes: Pre-selecting high-cost or controversial options that break trust.

Exceptions: Highly personal information like legal names or passwords.

Section 2: Onboarding \& Progress

Rule 3: Leverage the Goal Gradient Effect

Rule Name: Artificial Head Start

.

Explanation: Reframing the starting point of a task so the user feels they have already made progress toward a goal

.

Why it matters psychologically: People move faster toward a goal as they get closer to finishing it; starting at 20% creates "momentum" while starting at 0% feels "deflating"

.

Practical implementation guidance: Never start a progress bar at zero

. Count the very first interaction (like clicking "Get Started" or "Create Account") as Step 1

.

Good example: A car wash loyalty card with 10 stamps where 2 are already filled in, leading to double the completion rate of an 8-stamp empty card

.

Bad example: An onboarding screen showing "0% complete" with five empty steps

.

Common mistakes: Making the "head start" feel fake or unearned without linking it to a user action.

Exceptions: High-security settings where every step must be explicitly initiated by the user.

Rule 4: Refreame Onboarding as Continuous Momentum

Rule Name: The Profile Strength Meter

.

Explanation: Using visual indicators to show users how their previous actions have already contributed to their progress

.

Why it matters psychologically: It transforms a "separate event" like account creation into a milestone within a larger journey, making it harder to drop off

.

Practical implementation guidance: Include a profile strength meter that is active from the moment of sign-up

.

Good example: LinkedIn’s profile strength meter which is never at zero

.

Bad example: Requiring a full profile setup before the user can see the main interface.

Common mistakes: Using a progress bar that doesn't move after significant user effort.

Exceptions: Short, one-time utility flows where a meter adds unnecessary clutter.

Section 3: Trust \& Conversion

Rule 5: Establish Reciprocity through Value-First Design

Rule Name: Reciprocity Principle

.

Explanation: Providing the user with tangible value before asking them to commit (sign up, pay, or provide data)

.

Why it matters psychologically: Receiving a "gift" or value creates an unconscious debt and a deep human instinct to return the favor

.

Practical implementation guidance: Allow users to use the product or see results before showing a signup wall

. Offer "lite" versions of reports or tools for free

.

Good example: Costco free samples, Spotify 30-day trials, or Notion allowing full product use before payment

.

Bad example: A website that asks for an email address to see the results of a scan the user just performed

.

Common mistakes: Asking for a credit card before showing the "menu" of services

.

Exceptions: Products with high per-user operational costs.

Section 4: User Investment \& Retention

Rule 6: Foster the IKEA \& Endowment Effects

Rule Name: Investment-Driven Retention

.

Explanation: Encouraging users to customize, build, or make choices within the app before finalizing an account

.

Why it matters psychologically: The IKEA Effect dictates that people value things they built themselves more than pre-assembled items

. The Endowment Effect means simply feeling like one "owns" something makes it harder to give up

.

Practical implementation guidance: Shift sign-up to the end of a flow after the user has customized their experience

. Use "Continue" instead of "Sign Up" on buttons to emphasize progress over administrative tasks

.

Good example: Duolingo letting users pick a language, set a goal, and finish a lesson before asking for an account

.

Bad example: A standard "Email/Password/Sign Up" page that contains nothing belonging to the user

.

Common mistakes: Not saving the user's progress if they leave the flow before signing up.

Exceptions: Apps requiring immediate identity verification (e.g., banking).

Section 5: Motivation \& Framing

Rule 7: Utilize Loss Aversion for Motivation

Rule Name: Negative Framing

.

Explanation: Framing actions in terms of what the user will lose if they don't act, rather than what they will gain

.

Why it matters psychologically: The pain of losing something is psychologically twice as powerful as the pleasure of gaining it

. Humans have a "Status Quo Bias" and are wired to protect what they already have

.

Practical implementation guidance: When asking a user to upgrade or act, show the specific assets or data at risk

. Replace generic dismissals (e.g., "Maybe later") with high-stakes choices (e.g., "I'll risk it")

.

Good example: A storage app showing the names of files that will be lost if the user doesn't upgrade

.

Bad example: A storage app showing a list of "Premium Features" with an "Upgrade Now" button

.

Common mistakes: Overusing threats to the point of causing user anxiety or "dark pattern" fatigue.

Exceptions: Branding that must remain exclusively positive and aspirational.

Section 6: Value Perception

Rule 8: Apply the Contrast Effect for Relative Evaluation

Rule Name: Anchoring and Contrast

.

Explanation: Presenting a high-value item first to make subsequent costs seem smaller by comparison

.

Why it matters psychologically: The brain does not perform absolute evaluations; it performs relative ones based on the first number it sees (the "anchor")

.

Practical implementation guidance: Never show a cost in isolation

. Show a primary high-cost item before a secondary low-cost add-on

. Express costs as small percentages of a total

.

Good example: A $50 protection plan shown immediately after a $1,900 laptop purchase, labeled as "just 2.6%"

.

Bad example: Showing a $50 monthly protection plan on its own page where the user calculates the $600 yearly cost in isolation

.

Common mistakes: Placing the low-cost item first, which makes the high-cost item feel even more expensive.

Exceptions: Statutory price disclosures that must be presented plainly.

AI Design Constitution

Always prioritize psychological impact (conversion, retention, behavior) over purely aesthetic visual trends like "glass effects"

.

Always pre-select the most common choices for every field to reduce cognitive load

.

Always provide value through reciprocity before demanding user data or sign-ups

.

Always give users an artificial head start in onboarding; never start a progress meter at 0%

.

Always control the first number a user sees to anchor their perception of subsequent costs

.

Never present users with a blank state or empty forms when a smart default is known

.

Never frame a critical action purely as a "gain" if it can be framed as a "loss" of something the user already owns

.

Never make the signup screen the first interaction; let the user build something worth staying for first

.

Prefer "Continue" or action-oriented button text over administrative terms like "Sign Up" or "Search"

.

Prefer industry-proven patterns from successful products (e.g., using resources like Mobbin) over guessing what "good UX" looks like

.

Ensure that every choice offered to the user is necessary; minimize options to prevent decision fatigue

.

Ensure the user feels ownership of their progress (Endowment Effect) before they reach a conversion wall

.

Maximize the user's perceived momentum by counting their initial entry into the app as completed progress

.

Reduce the friction of decision-making by providing smart recommendations as defaults

.

Simplify complex tasks by breaking them into steps where the first step is already checked off

.

Prioritize demonstrating "why" a design decision works based on psychology rather than just "how" it looks

.
